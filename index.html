Drone-Controlled Pick-and-Place Robot

View CAD Assembly ↗

Design Challenge: I was tasked to create a robot within a 14" cube that can pick a ping pong ball from the ground, and place it in any of 9 designated spots (shown in assembly). I designed and welded the entire chassis together from sheet metal, hand-cut and fitted each piece. I then designed and printed several parts to mount electronics and an arm to the chassis. The electronics use an Arduino microcontroller with a buck converter, motor controller and battery pack for power. Two brushed DC motors drive the rear wheels, and two servo motors control arm movement and the gripper — which I designed and 3D printed. With only one servo left to operate the gripper, I decided to make a three claw mechanism, driven from a part that spins in the middle, and by gravity. When the claw spins in one direction, it locks the claws closed, allowing you to lift the ping ping ball without being dropped. I regretfully did not get any footage of the arm in action, however you can see it's use in the assembly.

Control: The robot is operated live using a standard drone controller paired to the Arduino via an RF receiver. I coded each joystick to map to a motor and servo. Then using a switch on the controler, I made a toggle between motor or servo control, making it intuitive to operate even with multiple moving parts.

What I learned: Hands-on experience with Arduinos, motors, servo control, battery-powered systems, sheet metal fabrication, and managing multiple actuators from a single microcontroller. This project was a great opportunity to combine my CAD, 3D printing, electronics, and fabrication skills, as well as apply mechanical design principles to create a robot capable of performing a specific task within defined constraints.

Fully Mechanical Excavator

View CAD Assembly ↗

Fully mechanical design: This model is entirely powered by hand and transmitted through 3D-printed gears, string, spring, nuts, bolts, and a bevel gear system. There are zero motors or servos, just mechanical advantage.

3 degrees of freedom: The arm can raise/lower, extend/retract, and open/close the bucket. The entire excavator swivels on its base via a bevel gear drive. Each motion is independently controllable by twisting or pulling different levers connected to the Chassis.

What I learned: Gear ratios, bevel gear geometry, spring mechanics, and creating multiple mechanisms in a compact space. This project pushed me to think about mechanical advantage in ways that I preaviously had not, like how every motion has a cost in terms of force and travel distance. It also forced me to think and plan for a big assembly, to succesfully bring together 30+ parts.

Bluetooth Speaker Rebuild

View Speaker CAD Assembly ↗ View Amplifier CAD Assembly ↗

Salvage + redesign: Before trashing my broken flat-screen TV, I opened it up and took the drivers that were too good to throw away. I rewired them into a standalone Bluetooth-enabled stereo system using a mini hi-fi amplifier.

Custom enclosure: I modeled the entire driver and amplifier in CAD prior to creating the enclosure and mounting bracket for the amplifier. This allowed me to accommodate the driver sizes, wiring, and screw hole layoout, so that I could make a fitting enclosure. I then 3D printed the enclosure in two sections that come together down the middle with dovetail connections for assembly and size restrictions. From there I just had to mirror the enclosure and print again for the other driver. The enclosure design also acts as an acoustic chamber to improve bass response. I created the Mounting bracket and knobs for the amplifier using interference fits and added mounting holes on the side.

What I learned: Audio basics (driver impedance, crossovers), Bluetooth module configuration, and how to design an enclosure that's both functional and printable. This project provided great practice in measuring and modeling objects in CAD for use as references in design.
